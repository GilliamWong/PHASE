Notes on Q-OIL:
1. As written Q-OIL is a non propagating reward on human actions + fixed weight behavior cloning
	 - "encouraging the agent to revisit ... intervention states" in the abstract, "softly biases exploration to regions that humans guide the robot toward" in section 1, and figure 1
	 - Q opt bootstraps from Q td, and with the BC term the loss function only makes the policy favor human actions in human states, while imitations does raise visitation to human states, there is no value based incentive to reach those states. 
	 - If successful trajectories are similar to the state space of demos, then this devolves into soft BC + BC and not any bonuses to exploration
		 - I saw this behavior in my data collection when I noticed that Q-OIL had a strong tendency to copy my behaviors, and not the other methods
	 - I think this is bad because 1. one of the main claims in the abstract of the paper is wrong, and 2. in simpler tasks like lift and can where successful trajectories are very similar this works because you're making the robot copy human interventions more and more over time, but what about in harder tasks where this is less possible? Single examples of unbuttoning a shirt or water bottle are in my opinion not strong evidence for whether this generalizes to much harder tasks with a much larger state distribution.
	 - Once the robot is on a human trajectory, the BC reg and bonus term tend to keep it there. Nothing gives the policy a value based reason to enter human states, and, when in a human state, copying the human action is in my view not the same as increasing exploration, even if we later anneal. 
	 - I'm also a bit confused on the main idea: if human interventions are suboptimal, why copy the action of the intervention instead of just the state? 
 2. "no asymptotic bias" is also wrong as stated - the fixed weight regularized BC term is asymptotic bias. If the main contribution of the paper is Q-OIL without BC then the BC regularization term should not be so prominently placed there and it should be made clear that it's not a core contribution
	 - But, in all of the text in the paper it looks like they're saying Q-OIL as a whole (not Q-OIL without BC, which is the term they use for the no BC reg version) is the thing they're presenting. I would be highly suspect if Q-OIL without BC does much worse than normal Q-OIL until you add another BC term (see first point)
3. If the main contribution of the paper is Q-OIL without BC, then it certainly isn't framed that way:
	 - abstract: "the right design choices, including ... imitation learning regularization"
	 - section 5.1: defined the objective with the BC term
	 - Table 2: BC weight is under "Q-OIL specific" hyperparameters
	 - Every single real world result uses BC. The only no BC results are in sim and in figure 7, which show that the no BC version collapses in can (also not addressed) and that not having BC does somewhat better in Lift for half the interventions. 
	 - If indeed BC is an engineering term and not critical to the overall contribution, why not frame it as such?
	 - The headline reported results all use BC - if the novel part of the paper is Q-OIL without BC then I would ideally like to see how the method performs on it's own, not with BC added on top. What is the contribution of Q-OIL itself? There are no non BC results on real hardware for this. See item 10. 
	 - If BC speeds up convergence then why does it do worse on lift? Simple task, sure, but not addressed. 
 4. The paper says that uniform replay samples bonus transitions in proportion to the intervention rate, but as stated this is also wrong - the buffer is cumulative so this tracks the share of interventions in the buffer and not the instantaneous intervention rate, which is what rho_t and the authors imply. Annealing if it happens would come from diluting the buffer over time or evicting from the buffer, but the hyperparameters set the buffer at 1e6 and so this is never tested in practice.
 5. Even if Q is between 0 and 10 the BC term can still dominate. There is no testing on whether it does or what happens if it does. 
	 - However in section 4 the log used for the suboptimality metric has the denominator go negative when n* < 229, which would make worse moves look better. Was the reference critic trained with the same reward or not
6. If the numbers reported for alpha in the gating model are correct, then the simulated human is about 3.5x more likely to intervene than real humans. Alpha = Pr(intervene | stagnant), which table 1's precision estimates. Table 1 reports precision as 0.175, and recall as 0.606. However table 2 then defines alpha as 0.599, 3.5x higher. 
	 - Section 4.2 says that k, delta, alpha, and beta were grid searched. But appendix F.1 says the grid search only covered k and delta. So how were the others found? 
7. Finding 3 says that human corrections follow different solution modes, so treating them as supervision can "induce undesirable multi modality". In table 2 the method then applies MLE BC with a gaussian head which is the definition of mode averaging. 
8. The ablation is a strawman in my view, the variant that has no decoupling still has a fixed reward bonus term, so I would expect it to fail at high b. I think a better version would be a single critic with a bonus that decays with the intervention rate, as in R-max. 
9. In figure 7, Q-OIL without BC shows a collapse in can performance and error bars from about 20-80%. This is not addressed, even though Q-OIL without BC is the main contribution of the paper, according to Pranav. 
	 - Additionally in lift, Q OIL without BC does better at most places than Q oil with BC, and with half the interventions. The reason for this is never explained, and in fact they say that BC regularization helps convergence.
10. In figure 10, the bonus sweep is shown as flat across a 50x range. If the bonus itself does nothing in a 50x range then I'm not sure what it's doing entirely. 
	 - Theory: the first term acts as another BC term instead of encouraging exploration. No change in performance across a 50x range fits this exactly. If the human action in a position is already ranked first among all other actions, then upweighting it even more would not change what the policy chooses to do. And so, we would expect to see a flat plateau of performance. Which is exactly what we see.
	 - In fact, Q-OIL without BC improves as the bonus is increased, which is exactly what I would expect if the bonus was acting as a BC substitute. When the BC regularization term is there, the bonus doesn't do much, when it's absent, it's actually working. 
	 - Thinking about it, if the first term in the L_theta loss function is basically behavior cloning, then removing the bonus reward changes this to BC+RL, as the paper states. Adding it back is just, BC + BC. 
		 - Therefore, you could test removing the bonus reward but upweighting lambda and seeing what happens. If it just is the extra BC push making the policy better then you would expect that upweighting lambda would do the same thing. 
11. Four participants per method is not enough for any statistical power. More than 4 is hard, yes, but in that case you would want paired comparisons. 
	 - A lead in performance at every checkpoint does not mean that the algorithm is ahead necessarily, they're basically one repeated comparison. 
12. Real robot results were run with a single person and ten eval episodes per checkpoint. The claimed gains are between 10-50% but binomial standard error over ten episodes is approx 0.16. For one of the tasks baselines were also run for more steps.
13. Error bars in figure 9 are across evals of ten episodes not across different training runs. 
14. pi*, Q*, and V* are not described, we do not know their datasets, what participants trajectories were included, what participants were trained or tested on, whether interventions were included, how it was trained, etc. Since these are used for most of the findings in the start of the paper, these should be included. 
	 - If demonstrations are used in the RLPD critics buffer from the same humans that intervened, then the demonstrations are in distribution where the interventions are not. Differences in timestep, action, etc, would all mean the critic labels interventions much more poorly. 
15. For finding 2, do we know if the critic is aligned with the human action distribution? The reference critic was presumably trained mostly on smooth policy actions, SpaceMouse actions are axis-aligned, saturated and jerky, so they may be out of distribution for it. If human movements are OOD for the critic, then it would be expected that the critic rates humans as suboptimal. 
	 - Similarly, training on one set of humans and then testing the critic on another set introduces a similar problem. 
	 - Overall the claim of broad suboptimality is, I think, overbroad. 
16. PAM models show when the human intervenes but not how. Sim results don't align with actual human interventions in that case, as we see with the differences in performance and why BC looks fine in sim but then doesn't help on lift with real humans
17. For findings 1-3, data collection is done with HIL, not Q-OIL. Everything in section 4 and the main motivation of the paper are not addressed with the new method. Intervention behavior is dependent on the algorithm (the paper itself says this is true for HIL, which learned slowly from corrections). This is a confound. 
	 - PAM is fit to how people respond to a single method, HIL, then used to evaluate every single other method in the paper. 
	 - For finding 3, human actions are compared against the converged HIL policy, which does not imitate. Obviously behavior would then drift away from human corrections. Interventions go into the replay buffer while Q-OIL imitates them directly. 
18. Figure 2 is cited two different ways in two different places: Finding 2 says that corrections "don't consistently increase V*" whereas section 5.1 justifies BC by the value improvement shown in figure 2. 
19. Finding 3 is circular. Q*(s, a_final) is compared to Q*(s, a_human). A policy trained with off policy RL seems likely to score better under a critic trained the same way, compared to noisy humans. The explanation of divergent solution modes is not tested or quantified. 
20. Ideally counterfactual could be, taking the robot at the state of intervention, rolling it out, and counting steps to success or total success rate. That would estimate n(s,a) monte carlo much better than using the critic on unfamiliar actions. 
21. Appendix D.2 and section 6.2 don't line up on the number of participants and methods. The appendix leaves out Q-OIL without BC, which is included in figure 7.
